TL;DR: Instead of committing a representation autoencoder to one hand-picked layer fusion, train the decoder and the diffusion transformer on random subsets of encoder layers. One decoder then works for any fusion, and swapping it in improves generation with no architecture change and no extra inference cost.

Key Findings

1

Layer fusion is a training distribution, not a hyperparameter. Averaging a random non-empty subset of encoder layers per sample places every fusion in the training support, instead of committing the decoder and generator to one fixed aggregate.

2

One decoder serves every readout. A single FuseReg decoder matches or outperforms decoders trained for a specific fusion, on the full k = 23 aggregate, the last k = 7 layers, and a single layer ℓ11.

3

Two stages, two rates. Reconstruction and generation prefer different amounts of regularization, so the decoder and the DiT get separate rates. Regularizing both gives the best generation: 13.96 → 9.93 gFID on DiT-Base.

1. Overview

Representation autoencoders (RAE[1], RAEv2[2]) replace the VAE with a frozen vision encoder, and fuse several encoder layers into one latent. That latent has two jobs at once: shallow layers preserve pixel detail for reconstruction, while deeper layers are easier for the generator to model. Picking one fusion hard-codes the trade-off into both stages.

FuseReg turns the choice into a training distribution. Each training sample averages a random non-empty subset of encoder layers. The decoder learns to reconstruct from changing layer compositions, and the diffusion transformer learns from noisy subset representations while targeting the full-layer fusion. At inference the full-layer mean is used, so nothing about the architecture or the sampling cost changes.

2. Method

For each training sample we draw a random mask over the K encoder layers, keep at least one layer, and average the layers that survive. Averaging by the number of retained layers leaves the latent unchanged in expectation, so the randomization varies only the directions along which encoder layers disagree — which is exactly what a decoder tied to one fusion is brittle to.

The same idea applies to the generator: the DiT sees noisy subset representations and predicts the full-layer target. Because reconstruction and generation are different prediction problems, the two stages use separate rates, pdec and pdit.

  • No cost at inference. FuseReg changes only what the decoder and DiT see during training. The architecture, the latent size and the sampler are unchanged.

4. Analysis

The encoder is frozen throughout, so the gains come from how the decoder reads a fixed representation, not from better features. FuseReg reconstructs well from individual layers and small subsets, which is why improving the readout of a fixed representation also improves generation.

5. Conclusion

Layer fusion in a representation autoencoder does not have to be decided once and baked into the decoder and the generator. Training over random layer subsets gives one decoder that works across fusions, and gives the generator a latent interface it models more easily. The result is better generation at no additional inference cost, with the encoder left untouched.
